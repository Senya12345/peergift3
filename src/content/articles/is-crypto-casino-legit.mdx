Nearly every search that lands on this site is a version of one question. Is Rainbet
legit. Is BC.Game real. Should I trust Shuffle. Is it rigged.

The honest answer is almost never yes or no. Every casino we cover holds some kind of
licence and pays most withdrawals, and several of them have something on the record that
should change how much you put in. This is the order we check things in, with what turned
up at each step across the nine operators we rate.

## Start with the licence, then read past it

A licence number is the first thing to look for and the least informative thing you will
find. It tells you a regulator issued paperwork. It says nothing about how that regulator
treats a player who is owed money.

The jurisdiction tells you more than the number. [Stake](/casinos/stake/),
[Roobet](/casinos/roobet/), [Shuffle](/casinos/shuffle/) and [1win](/casinos/1win/) hold
Curaçao licences. [Rainbet](/casinos/rainbet/), [BC.Game](/casinos/bc-game/) and
[Duel](/casinos/duel/) are licensed in Anjouan, an island of the Comoros whose framework
carries noticeably lighter oversight. [Winna](/casinos/winna/) runs out of Costa Rica with
a supplementary licence from the Tobique Gaming Commission in New Brunswick, Canada.
[Polymarket](/casinos/polymarket/), a prediction market rather than a casino, has been
regulated in the US by the CFTC since November 2025.

Two things are worth doing with the number itself.

**Check it is stated consistently.** We could not settle 1win's. One source gives
OGL/2024/587/0621, another 8048/JAZ2018-040, and we found nothing saying which is current.
That proves nothing on its own, but a licence the operator does not state clearly is a
licence you cannot look up.

**Ask why the operator holds the licence it holds.** BC.Game used to be licensed in
Curaçao through a company called Small House B.V. In November 2024 a Curaçao court
declared that company bankrupt over more than $2 million in documented unpaid player
winnings. BC.Game disputed the ruling, left the Curaçao market the following month, and is
now licensed in Anjouan through Twocent Technology Limited. It still operates and still
pays. A move from a stricter regulator to a lighter one, straight after losing a case about
unpaid winnings, is the most useful single fact on its page.

## Read the gap between rating sites, not the average

Our scores combine the ratings several review platforms publish. The obvious way to use
them is the combined number. The more useful thing is how far apart the sources sit.

When platforms that measure different things agree, the picture is probably stable. When
they don't, one of them is seeing something the other isn't:

- **Winna** — AskGamblers player rating 3.7/10, Casino Guru Safety Index 9.3/10. Nearly
  six points apart on the same casino.
- **1win** — AskGamblers' player rating is 9.6/10. The same site's algorithmic CasinoRank
  is 2.8/10.
- **Shuffle** — AskGamblers' CasinoRank 5.6 against a player rating of 6.9, and a Casino
  Guru Safety Index of 4.8, which Casino Guru itself labels Low.
- **Roobet** — AskGamblers' algorithm and its players land at 5.1 and 5.3. Close together,
  and both middling. Agreement doesn't make a casino good. It makes the number believable.

Player ratings run high when a casino treats the people who stay and win small well.
Algorithmic scores weigh terms, licensing and how complaints end. A player score far above
the algorithm is often a problem that simply hasn't reached the reviewer yet.

How we combine and normalise the sources is on the [methodology page](/methodology/).

## Treat Trustpilot as a signal about Trustpilot first

It is the rating most people check and the one we lean on least, for a reason Trustpilot
keeps publishing itself: manipulated reviews. When we checked, BC.Game's rating was
suspended after a batch of fake reviews was removed. Roobet carried a guideline-breach
notice for the same thing. Rainbet showed 2.3/5 across 586 reviews, with a fake-review flag
on the same profile.

So when a crypto casino's Trustpilot score looks surprisingly good, read the top of the
profile for a notice before you read the number.

## "Is it rigged?" — what provably fair does and doesn't cover

Most of these casinos run their own dice, crash, plinko and mines games under a provably
fair label. The mechanism is real. Before a round the casino commits to a hashed server
seed, you contribute a client seed, and afterwards the unhashed seed is revealed so anyone
can recompute the result. If it matches, that round's outcome was not changed after your
bet went in.

That is narrower than the badge suggests. It checks one round against the rules in force.
It does not check the rules.

The allegation several unconnected reviewers have made about Winna sits exactly in that
gap: not that individual rounds were altered, but that the odds of large multipliers on
its in-house games were cut, along with the payout on the wins that did land. We could not
verify the mechanism. A provably fair check would not have caught it either way.

It also covers none of the third-party slots or live tables, and it has nothing to say
about where players actually lose money at these sites — which is the next section. The
casinos we list with verifiable in-house games are on the
[provably fair casinos](/provably-fair-casinos/) page.

## What happens after a big win

Read enough complaint records and one story keeps coming back. Small withdrawals clear in
minutes. A large one triggers a review, a new document request, a bonus-abuse finding, or
silence.

- BC.Game's bankruptcy case was that scenario at scale, and players still report large
  wins followed by account reviews or frozen balances.
- At Shuffle, one documented case had around $20,000 held in compliance review for roughly
  14 days against a string of generic replies.
- Winna players describe payments taking 36 to 50 days, with new verification requests
  arriving after an account had already been approved.
- Roobet's AskGamblers record shows disputed withdrawals averaging about $11,669 and around
  nine days to resolve — real, if milder than the others.

This is also where "no KYC" stops being true. A casino that skips verification at signup
can still demand it the moment a withdrawal is bigger than it expected. We covered that
threshold separately in [what "no KYC" actually means](/articles/crypto-casino-kyc-explained/).

The practical defence is dull and it works: verify the account before you win anything
large, read the withdrawal section of the terms, and cash out in amounts inside the limits
the casino actually publishes.

## Who is behind it

The check most people skip. A licence names a company. It doesn't always name people.

FinTelegram, an outlet that covers offshore gambling, has published pieces questioning
whether "John Evans", presented as Rainbet's CEO and co-founder, is an identifiable person,
and describing its split between a Curaçao registration and an Anjouan licence as
regulatory arbitrage. We could not verify that. We still think it belongs in the decision,
next to the fact that the licence paperwork itself checks out.

Stake, the largest brand here, carries a different kind of flag: Casinomeister lists it in
its Warning section over how it handles player complaints. That is about disputes, not
game fairness — which is to say, about exactly the situation in the previous section.

## The short version

Before a first deposit anywhere, we look for:

1. A licence number stated once, clearly, in a jurisdiction we can name.
2. Whether the licence history involves leaving a regulator.
3. The gap between player and algorithmic ratings rather than their average.
4. A notice at the top of the Trustpilot profile.
5. How complaints about large withdrawals end.

None of the casinos on this site clears all five. That is why each one is listed with its
criticism attached, and why we would rather you read the [full ranking](/) than trust any
single score, ours included. We earn a commission when readers sign up through our links.
It does not buy a position and it does not remove a finding.
